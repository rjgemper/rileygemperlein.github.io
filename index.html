By growing up in a household with two parents that met through the Peace Corps I was exposed to cultural practices, particularly Sesotho, an African language, they had engaged with while serving. The opportunity to immerse myself in these practices, evident through short exchanges of Sesotho with my mother and conversations of my fathers work in horticulture with the village kids, expanded my definition of community to extend across continents. Through these stories, I recognized the distinct methods communities employ to adapt to varying resource availability.

“I can’t help but think about all the women that came before me that didn’t have the opportunity to be recognised.”

- Carolyn Bertozzi, PhD

To explore the iterative design processes behind development of these methods I became a part of the Academy of Engineering and Advanced Manufacturing at my high school, Apex Friendship. My involvement in the program, through engagement with hands-on engineering projects, propelled an understanding of differences in design creation when resource constraints gird the process of technological development. To clarify the underpinnings of global design constraints, I conducted individual research in my final year of high school, under the mentorship of a trauma surgeon, Dr. Jared Gallaher, on trends of unsafe environmental exposure during surgery in South African countries. Following my identification of surgical tool corrosion through concentrated humidity exposure resulting in increased levels of surgical site infections, I iterated through designs of a low-cost, low-power portable solution which could be stored conjointly with surgical tools between operations following autoclaving. With this understanding of the health implications resultant from oxidative stress in hospital settings as my foundation, I presented my work to a panel of senior engineers at Becton Dickinson, a Fortune 500 company focused on the development of non-invasive surgical technology.

To expand my work in environmental health exposure identification and intervention development, I am now enrolled at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, where I intend to study Environmental Health Science. Through that major, I seek to understand the toxicological contributors to chronic disease, particularly amongst rural communities. By engaging in environmental chemical coursework at the Gillings School of Global Public Health, I will explore the intersection of chemical exposures with the downstream public health consequences. To apply this knowledge in a professional context, I am currently conducting research on a systematic review of literature discussing the metal contamination through water in Punjab, India and its relationship to identified patterns of cancerous outcomes in the area in the Fry Lab under the mentorship of Dr. Neha Sehgal.

Future Plans

Over the summer of 2027, I hope to join the research and development team at Becton Dickinson at their United States location in Arizona as an assistant researcher on their corporate stewardship team. This position would be focused on designing solutions to reduce current use of ethylene oxide sterilization through exploration into alternative modalities.

Similar to the processes which have guided my methods for supporting communities, the blueprint for my long-term goals remains in the iterative stages. However, in seeking a future in upstream research and development, my goal remains to posit beyond the observed downstream consequences, asking through what mechanisms do multifactorial exposure points influence community health outcomes?
